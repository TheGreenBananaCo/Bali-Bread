Why green banana flour?

A banana is mostly starch before it ripens and mostly sugar after. Harvest it green, dry it, and mill it, and you catch it on the starch side of that line. A large share of what you get is resistant starch — starch your small intestine cannot break down, so it reaches the colon intact and feeds the bacteria living there instead of feeding you. The flour itself is pale, fine, and faintly sweet until baked. It is not a wheat substitute and it does not pretend to be one.

We first came to green banana flour in Bali, Indonesia, after Milo was laid low by protozoa, picked up from unwashed fruit or a bad hold-down surfing at Canggu. Our friends there prescribed three things: Pocari Sweat, activated charcoal and green banana flour. We had never heard of it, though much of the world treats it as an everyday superfood. We learned to bake with it, and after half a decade of baking for friends and family we have a recipe list that could run a whole menu on this one flour — sourdough, pizza, buns, pancakes, waffles and more.

  • Feeds your gut bacteria. Resistant starch passes through the small intestine undigested, and in the colon your gut bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids, which help nourish the cells lining the gut.
  • Starch that digests slowly. Resistant starch is not broken down into sugar in the small intestine the way ordinary starch is.
  • Simple ingredients, minimally processed. Nothing fortified, nothing artificial.

The Science

What’s actually in the flour

These are the numbers for green banana flour itself, measured per 100g of flour. We’ve put them here rather than on the bread, because that’s where they honestly belong — see the note underneath.

40g

Resistant starch / 100g of flour

A fermentable fiber, and by far the highest concentration in anything we sell. Heat is what destroys it, so the flour is where it survives: stir it into a smoothie or overnight oats and it arrives the way it left the mill.

2×

Resistant starch, baked vs. ordinary bread

A 2025 trial on banana-flour bread measured 2.56% resistant starch against 1.32% in a conventional control loaf. Roughly double — and still a modest number in absolute terms, which is exactly why we tell you to buy the flour if resistant starch is what you are after.

1,491mg

Potassium / 100g of flour

Plus magnesium, phosphorus, and antioxidant polyphenols — naturally, with no fortification.

4

Flours in the loaf

Rice, green banana, tapioca and corn. That is the whole blend, and there is no wheat, rye or barley in it. We do not print the phrase “gluten-free” — in the US that is a regulated term meaning every batch tests under 20 parts per million. We bake it fresh here in Santa Barbara. If you have celiac disease, that distinction is the one that matters, and you should hear it from us.

An honest note about baking. Resistant starch in green bananas is the RS2 type, and RS2 is not heat-stable — a good deal of it converts during baking, and some re-forms as the loaf cools. So the numbers above describe the flour, not a slice of bread. If resistant starch is what you’re after, buy the flour and use it unbaked. If you want bread that happens to be made of something better than refined wheat, buy the loaf. We’d rather tell you that than let you assume.

Sources: CIQUAL/CNEVA (1993); Bezerra et al., 2013; Journal of Nutritional Science, 2022; Plants 14(2):207, 2025.

Frequently asked questions

Is there gluten in it?

No wheat, no rye, no barley, in anything we make. The flour blend is rice, green banana, tapioca and corn. What we deliberately do not print is the phrase “gluten-free” — in the United States that phrase is regulated, and it means every batch has been laboratory-tested below 20 parts per million. We bake it fresh here in Santa Barbara, and we do not lab-test batches, so the phrase is not ours to use. If you have celiac disease, read this as bread made without gluten grains, not as certified gluten-free bread. That difference is small on paper and large in a hospital.

What about other allergens?

Our pancake & waffle mix and quick-bread premix call for you to add one egg at home — the bag itself has no egg in it. Everything we sell is baked fresh here in Santa Barbara under California’s Cottage Food Operation law, not in a certified allergen-controlled facility, so we can’t guarantee any product is free of cross-contact with other allergens. If you have a serious allergy, message us before you order and we’ll tell you exactly what’s been in the kitchen.

Why only two bakes a week?

Gluten is the protein network that gives ordinary bread its structure, and it is part of what keeps a supermarket loaf soft for a week. Without it, the starch in the crumb recrystallises quickly — bakers call it retrogradation — and the loaf firms up inside three or four days. Baking Sunday and Wednesday means nothing we hand you is older than that. Day three is still very good toast.

How should I store it?

On the counter, in its bag, for two days. After that, slice the loaf and freeze it, then toast the slices from frozen. Do not put it in the fridge. Just above freezing is the temperature range where starch recrystallises fastest, so a refrigerator is the quickest way to stale bread there is.
